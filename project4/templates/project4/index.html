Preference elicitation

A user study comparing two ways of asking someone what films they like: repeatedly choosing between two, or ranking ten at a time. The study was designed but not run, as the project sheet specifies. What is here is the protocol, the model behind it, and the working instrument a participant would be given.

Task 3 · the design in one paragraph

What the study asks

Every participant does both designs, in a counterbalanced order, on disjoint sets of films. After each block they answer ten held out comparisons that the model never saw, which is the primary outcome: does the preference vector estimated from this interface predict choices the person had not yet made? Workload is rated after each block, and at the end they say which interface they would rather use again.

Planning

Simulating the study before running it

The model is fully specified, so the study can be simulated rather than guessed at. {{ sim.meta.participants }} synthetic participants with known taste vectors answered both designs at matched budgets, and the fitted vector was scored on {{ sim.meta.holdout_pairs }} held out comparisons each. Simulated participants answer noisily, so the ceiling is {{ sim.ceiling }} rather than 1.

Matched on decisions made, ranking wins clearly: a ranking of ten is nine choices, and each of those choices is made against a wider field than a pairwise comparison, so it is worth more. Matched on the participant's time, the advantage nearly vanishes, because a ranking of ten costs far more than nine times a single comparison.

That is the whole reason this study is worth running, and it is what fixes the design: the time matched effect is about {{ sim.power.effect }} accuracy, which needs roughly {{ sim.power.within_total_80 }} participants within subjects and {{ sim.power.between_per_group_80 }} per group between subjects. The second number is why nobody should run this between participants.
